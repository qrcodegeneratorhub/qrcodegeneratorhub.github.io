QR Codes for Events: Tickets, Check-In and Feedback

From the invitation to the feedback form, QR codes make events run smoother — if they are tested before the doors open.

Events are a natural home for QR codes: a ticket on a phone, a check-in at the door, a programme that never needs reprinting, and a feedback form that catches people before they leave. Used well, codes remove queues, paper and confusion. Used badly, they create a bottleneck at the entrance and a room full of people staring at a code that will not scan. The difference is preparation: the right code, in the right place, tested in advance. This guide covers the event uses that work and the checks that keep them working on the day.

Tickets and check-in

A QR code on a ticket — printed or on a phone — lets staff check people in with a quick scan instead of searching a paper list. Each code should be unique to the ticket, so it can be scanned once and then marked as used, which prevents a copied ticket from admitting two people. Test the scanning setup at the venue before the doors open, in the actual lighting at the entrance, and have a backup: a printed guest list and a charged spare phone. Keep the queue moving by placing the scanner where people naturally pause, and make the code large and high contrast on the ticket. For a large event, use more than one check-in point so a single slow scanner does not become a bottleneck.

Programmes, maps and schedules

A QR code on a poster or a badge linking to the event programme means the schedule can change without reprinting anything — a speaker drops out, a room changes, and the online programme updates instantly. The same code can link to a venue map, a speaker biography, or a live poll. Put the code where people look for information: at the entrance, on the back of the badge, and on the screens between sessions. Keep the destination simple and fast, because the venue Wi-Fi may be slow and the mobile signal may be weak. And keep a few printed programmes for the people whose phones are dead or who prefer paper.

Networking and contact exchange

A QR code on a badge or a business card that links to a vCard or a LinkedIn profile turns a brief conversation into a saved contact. It is faster than dictating a number and more reliable than a card that gets lost in a pocket. Encourage speakers to put a contact code on their final slide, so the audience can reach them after the talk. For exhibitors, a code that opens a lead-capture form — name, email, and a note about what they want — is more useful than a pile of business cards, because the leads arrive already typed and organised. Just make sure the form is short: every extra field loses people.

Feedback while people are still there

The best time to ask for feedback is before people leave, while the event is fresh. A QR code on the exit, on the chairs, or on the final slide that links to a two-minute feedback form will get far more responses than an email sent the next day. Keep the form short — three questions and a comment box — and tell people it takes two minutes, because they will not start a form that looks long. Read the feedback the same day, while you still remember the event, and use it to fix the things that went wrong before the next one. A feedback code costs nothing and is the cheapest way to improve an event.

What to test before the doors open

Test every code at the venue, at the size and in the lighting where it will be used, on two different phones. Test the check-in flow with a real ticket, including a ticket that has already been scanned, to make sure the system rejects the second scan. Test the programme link on the venue's Wi-Fi, not just on your office connection. Charge the scanning phones, bring chargers and a spare device, and brief the door staff on the backup plan if a scanner fails. Events are unforgiving: a code that fails at the door fails in front of everyone, at the worst possible moment. An hour of testing the day before prevents all of it.

Frequently asked questions

Can one QR code admit two people?

Only if the check-in system allows it. Use unique codes per ticket and a scanner that marks each code as used after the first scan.

Should event programmes be QR codes or paper?

Both. A QR code keeps the programme current, and a few printed copies help people with dead phones or no signal.

How do I get feedback with a QR code?

Put a code linking to a short form at the exit and on the final slide, and tell people it takes two minutes.
