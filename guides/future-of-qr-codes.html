The Future of QR Codes: What Comes Next

From restaurant menus to product passports, QR codes are spreading into places barcodes never reached. Where the technology is heading.

QR codes were invented in 1994 to track car parts in a factory. Thirty years later they are on menus, tickets, payments, packaging and posters, and their use is still growing. The pandemic made scanning a habit for billions of people, and the technology has not stood still since: codes are getting smarter, more standardised, and more deeply woven into how products, payments and identities work. This guide looks at where QR codes are heading, what will change, and what will stay the same.

From barcodes to product passports

The biggest change coming is in retail. The global standards body that governs barcodes has set a goal for two-dimensional codes — QR codes and their cousins — to replace traditional barcodes on products, a transition known as Sunrise 2027. The reason is capacity: a QR code on a product can carry the price, the expiry date, the batch number, allergen information, and a link to the product's story, all in one code. The European Union is going further, with digital product passports that will require products to carry a code linking to information about their materials, repairability and recycling. The humble product code is becoming a doorway to the product's whole history.

Payments without cards or terminals

QR payments are already the norm in much of Asia, and they are spreading. The next step is interoperability: a single code that works with any bank or payment app, rather than a different code for each system. Central banks in several countries are building shared QR payment systems, and cross-border QR payments — paying a merchant in another country by scanning their local code — are already being piloted. For small merchants, this means accepting digital payments with nothing but a printed code. For customers, it means paying abroad without exchanging cash or carrying a card. The card terminal is not disappearing, but the printed code is becoming a serious alternative.

Identity, tickets and access

QR codes are increasingly used as proof: a ticket, a boarding pass, a vaccination record, a membership card, a door key. The next generation adds security: codes that change every few seconds, codes that are signed so they cannot be forged, and codes tied to a person's device so a screenshot cannot be reused. Digital identity wallets, now being rolled out in several countries, use QR codes as the bridge between a phone and the real world — proving your age, your ticket, or your right to enter, without revealing more than necessary. The code becomes not just a link, but a credential.

Smarter codes, same pattern

The QR code pattern itself is unlikely to change much — its strength is that every phone already reads it. What will change is what sits behind it: richer destinations, better design tools, and codes that adapt to context, such as showing a different page depending on the language of the scanning phone. Augmented reality may add a layer on top: scan a code and see a 3D model of the product, or directions overlaid on the street. But the core interaction will stay the same, because it is already as simple as it can be: point, scan, done. Technologies that replace the QR code will have to beat that simplicity, and so far none has.

What this means for you

For most people, the future of QR codes means using them more, in more places, with more confidence. Learn the safety habit — check where a code leads before you tap — because the more codes you scan, the more it matters. If you run a business, start using static codes now for the things that will not change, and keep an eye on dynamic codes for campaigns you want to measure. And remember that the code itself is free and open: nobody owns the QR code, and no company can take it away. That openness is why it spread, and why it will keep spreading into corners of life that its inventors never imagined.

Frequently asked questions

Will QR codes replace barcodes?

In retail, gradually. The industry target is for two-dimensional codes to replace traditional barcodes on products, starting with the Sunrise 2027 transition.

Will QR codes still exist in ten years?

Almost certainly. Every phone reads them, they cost nothing, and no simpler alternative has emerged.

What is a digital product passport?

A QR code on a product linking to information about its materials, repairability and recycling — a requirement being introduced in the European Union.
