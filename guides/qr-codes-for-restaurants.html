QR Codes for Restaurants: Menus, Ordering and Reviews

Table menus, order-ahead, feedback and loyalty — how restaurants use QR codes to serve more customers with less paper.

Restaurants were among the first businesses to adopt QR codes at scale, and for good reason: a code on a table solves several problems at once. Menus never need reprinting, orders arrive faster, bills can be paid without waiting, and feedback is collected while the meal is still fresh in the customer's mind. But a QR code done badly — a tiny code, a slow website, a forced app download — drives customers away faster than a paper menu ever did. This guide covers the uses that work, the mistakes to avoid, and how to introduce QR codes without alienating the customers who prefer paper.

Table menus that actually work

The most common restaurant QR code links to the menu, and the difference between a good one and a bad one is the experience after the scan. A good menu loads fast on mobile data, is readable without pinching and zooming, shows prices clearly, and marks allergens and dietary options. A bad menu is a PDF of the print menu, slow to download and impossible to read on a phone. Build a simple mobile web page for your menu rather than linking to a PDF, keep it updated when dishes change, and put the code on a sturdy table tent that can be wiped clean. Keep a few paper menus available too — some customers prefer them, and a restaurant that forces a phone on everyone loses goodwill.

Ordering and paying from the table

A QR code can do more than show the menu: it can open an ordering page tied to the table number, so customers order and pay without waiting for a server. This works best for fast-casual restaurants, cafes and bars, where speed matters more than table service. The system should be simple: scan, browse, order, pay, and the kitchen receives the order with the table number. Make sure the payment step is quick and offers the common options, and make sure a human is still available — a customer with a question or a problem should be able to wave down a server rather than fight with a web page. The goal is to remove waiting, not to remove people.

Reviews and feedback while the meal is fresh

The best moment to ask for a review is just after the meal, while the customer is still at the table and feeling good about it. A QR code on the bill or the table tent that links directly to your Google review page will generate far more reviews than a request made days later by email. Keep the ask short — Scan to tell us how we did — and link straight to the review form, not to your homepage. You can also link to a short feedback form for complaints, which is better than a public one-star review: a customer who can report a problem privately is a customer you can still win back. Read the feedback weekly and act on the patterns you see.

Loyalty, specials and takeaway

A QR code can run a loyalty scheme without a stamp card: customers scan a code at each visit, and after a set number of visits they earn a reward. The code can also advertise the day's special, link to a takeaway ordering page, or offer a discount for a first online order. For takeaway, put the code on the bag or the box, linking to a reorder page or a feedback form — the customer is holding your packaging at home, which is the perfect moment to invite them back. Keep the rewards simple and visible: a free coffee after eight visits is easy to understand, while a points scheme with tiers and expiry dates is not.

Mistakes that drive customers away

The biggest mistake is forcing the QR code on everyone and removing the paper menu entirely; always offer a choice. The second is a code that is too small, faded, or placed where the light is poor — test it at every table, not just one. The third is a destination that demands an app download, an account, or personal details before showing the menu; customers will abandon it. The fourth is a menu that is out of date, which destroys trust instantly. And the fifth is forgetting that not every customer has a charged phone or a data connection: keep paper menus, keep a human available, and treat the QR code as an option that makes the meal better, not as a replacement for hospitality.

Frequently asked questions

Do customers prefer QR menus or paper menus?

Most are happy with either, but many prefer a choice. Keep paper menus available for customers who want them.

Can QR codes take orders and payments?

Yes. A code linked to an ordering page can take orders and payments by table number, which suits fast-casual restaurants best.

How do QR codes help a restaurant get reviews?

A code on the bill linking directly to the review page catches customers while the meal is fresh, which generates far more reviews.
