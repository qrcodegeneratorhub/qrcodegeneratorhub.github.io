Are QR Codes Safe? How to Spot a Malicious QR Code

QR codes are convenient — and criminals know it. Learn how QR scams work and how to protect yourself in ten seconds.

A QR code is a shortcut: one scan replaces typing a long address or password. That convenience is exactly what makes QR codes attractive to criminals, because a code hides its destination. You cannot tell by looking at a square of black and white modules whether it leads to a restaurant menu or to a fake login page designed to steal your password. Attacks using QR codes — sometimes called quishing, short for QR phishing — are rising fast. The good news is that a few seconds of checking before you tap will defeat almost all of them.

How QR code scams work

The most common scam is the swapped code: a criminal prints a sticker with a malicious QR code and pastes it over a genuine one on a parking meter, a restaurant table, a poster or a parcel locker. You scan what looks like the legitimate code and land on a fake payment page that asks for your card details. A second scam is the phishing code in an email: the message contains a QR code instead of a link, because email security filters check links but often ignore images, so the malicious code slips through. A third is the fake prize or delivery code, which promises a refund or a gift and asks you to log in or download an app. All three rely on the same thing: you tapping before you look.

The ten-second check before you tap

Your phone shows you the destination before you open it — use that moment. After scanning, read the address in the notification: does it match the business you expect, is it spelled correctly, and does it start with https? A parking payment that leads to a strange domain, a bank login that leads to a misspelled address, or a menu that asks you to download an app should all stop you. If the address looks wrong, do not tap it. Instead, type the business's real address yourself, or use its official app. This one habit — reading before tapping — defeats the vast majority of QR scams, because the fake destination is almost always visibly wrong to someone who looks.

Codes that ask for money or logins

Be especially careful with any QR code that asks for payment, a password, or personal details. A genuine parking meter will take you to the operator's own payment page; a fake one will take you to a convincing copy that steals your card number. A genuine restaurant menu will simply show the menu; a fake one may ask you to log in with your social media account first, which hands the attacker your credentials. As a rule, never enter payment details or passwords immediately after scanning a code you found in a public place. If you need to pay, use the official app or type the address yourself. And never download an app because a QR code told you to — get apps only from your phone's official store.

What to do if you scanned a bad code

If you tapped a suspicious code, do not panic, but act quickly. If you entered a password, change it immediately from the real website, and change it everywhere else you used the same one. If you entered card details, call your bank and ask them to watch the card or replace it. If you downloaded an app, delete it and run your phone's security scan. If the code was on a parking meter or a restaurant table, tell the business — they need to know their code was replaced, and your report protects the next person. Keep an eye on your accounts for a few days for unfamiliar activity. Quick action turns a close call into a minor incident.

Safe scanning habits for everyday life

Make safe scanning a habit, not a chore. Prefer codes from sources you trust — a code printed inside a shop is safer than one stuck on a lamppost. Be suspicious of codes that appear in unexpected places, such as an email from a company you do not use, or a sticker placed over another code — look for the edges of a sticker on top of a printed code. Keep your phone updated, because updates fix the vulnerabilities that malicious codes target. And teach children the same rule you teach yourself: read where it goes before you tap. QR codes are a genuinely useful technology, and a few seconds of care is all it takes to keep them that way.

Frequently asked questions

Can a QR code give my phone a virus?

A QR code itself is just data, but it can lead to a malicious website or a fake app download. Check the destination before tapping, and only install apps from the official store.

How can I tell if a QR code is fake?

Look at the address your phone shows after scanning. A misspelled domain, an unexpected address, or a demand for payment or a login are all warning signs.

Are QR codes on parking meters safe?

Usually, but check for a sticker placed over the original code, and verify the payment address matches the parking operator before paying.
