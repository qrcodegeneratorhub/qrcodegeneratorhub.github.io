QR Codes vs Barcodes: What Is the Difference?

One holds a number, the other holds a message. How the two codes work, why shops use one and posters use the other, and whether QR codes will replace barcodes.

Barcodes and QR codes look like cousins — both are patterns that a machine reads — but they are built for different jobs. A barcode holds a small amount of data, usually a product number, and is read by a laser scanner at a till. A QR code holds far more data, can be read by any phone camera, and can survive damage that would kill a barcode. Understanding the difference helps you choose the right one, and explains why supermarkets still use barcodes while restaurants use QR codes. This guide compares them side by side.

How a barcode works

A traditional barcode is a series of vertical bars of varying widths, encoding a number — usually a product code such as a UPC or EAN. A laser scanner reads the widths of the bars and looks the number up in a database, which returns the price and the product name. The barcode itself contains almost no information; it is a key, and the database holds the meaning. That design is fast and cheap, which is why it dominates retail: a barcode can be printed tiny, scanned in a fraction of a second, and read reliably by hardware that costs very little. Its weakness is capacity: a barcode holds at most a few dozen characters, and only in one dimension.

How a QR code works

A QR code stores data in two dimensions, as a grid of black and white modules, which is why it is called a two-dimensional code. It can hold thousands of characters — a web address, a paragraph of text, a contact card, a Wi-Fi password — and it includes error correction, so it still scans when part of it is damaged. It can be read by any phone camera, from any angle, without a laser scanner. The trade-off is size: a QR code needs to be larger than a barcode to hold the same readability, and it is slower for a till to read at high speed. That is why you will not see QR codes on every product at a supermarket checkout — yet.

Capacity, damage and reading distance

The practical differences come down to three things. Capacity: a QR code holds up to about 4,000 characters of text, while a barcode holds about 20 to 30. Damage: a barcode with a scratch across it is unreadable, because it has no redundancy, while a QR code can lose up to 30 percent of its pattern and still scan. Reading: a barcode needs a laser scanner aimed across the bars, while a QR code can be read by a phone from any angle, at a distance, and even from a screen. Those differences explain the division of labour: barcodes for fast, cheap identification at a till, QR codes for rich information in the hands of a customer.

Why shops still use barcodes

Supermarkets and warehouses use barcodes because they are optimised for speed and cost at scale. A cashier can scan a barcode in a fraction of a second without aiming carefully, the codes are tiny and cheap to print, and the scanners are durable and standardised. QR codes are slower at the till and larger on the packaging, which matters when a product is small. That said, the industry is moving: the global barcode standards body has set a target for QR-style codes to replace traditional barcodes on retail products, because a two-dimensional code can carry an expiry date, a batch number, and a link to product information alongside the price. The change will take years, but the direction is clear.

Which one should you use?

Use a barcode when you need fast, cheap identification in a system you control — a till, a warehouse, a library — where a scanner and a database are already in place. Use a QR code when the reader is a customer with a phone, when the data is rich or needs to survive damage, or when the code must work without a database lookup — a menu, a ticket, a Wi-Fi login, a contact card. For most small businesses and personal uses, the QR code is the right choice, because the reader already has the scanner in their pocket. The barcode is not going away, but the QR code is the code of the customer, and that is where its future lies.

Frequently asked questions

Can a QR code replace a barcode?

For many uses, yes — and the retail industry is moving that way. But barcodes are still faster and cheaper at a supermarket till.

How much data can a QR code hold?

Up to about 4,000 characters of text, depending on the type of data. A barcode holds only 20 to 30 characters.

Why can a damaged QR code still scan?

Because of error correction: redundant data in the code lets the scanner reconstruct up to 30 percent of a damaged pattern.
